\section{Manifold Topology and In-Context Out-of-Prior Detection}

Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) implement approximate Bayesian inference in their forward pass. When the input context dataset $D_c = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^T$ deviates from the training prior $\mathcal{P}_{\text{ID}}$, the network's behavior is mathematically undefined. To detect such out-of-prior (OOD) contexts, we examine the internal hidden representations of the model.

\subsection{Representation Extraction and Subspace Projection}
Let $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ($d=256$) be the sequence-averaged representation extracted from the final Transformer encoder layer (Layer 5) after processing the context dataset $D_c$. To isolate the structural prior boundary from high-dimensional observational noise, we project $z$ into a low-dimensional subspace using a non-linear projector $\Phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^2$ (implemented via UMAP).

Crucially, the projector $\Phi$ is fit jointly on a union of $N_{\text{cal}}$ in-distribution calibration samples and $N_{\text{ref}}$ out-of-prior reference samples:
\begin{equation}
X_{\text{fit}} = X_{\text{cal}} \cup X_{\text{ood\_ref}}
\end{equation}
This joint fitting prevents the collapse of OOD-discriminating dimensions that occurs when fitting only on in-distribution data.

\subsection{Mahalanobis Distance Metric}
In the projected space, the out-of-prior scoring function $S(D_c)$ is defined as the Mahalanobis distance relative to the projected calibration set:
\begin{equation}
S(D_c) = \sqrt{(\Phi(z) - \mu_{\text{cal}})^T \Sigma_{\text{cal}}^{-1} (\Phi(z) - \mu_{\text{cal}})}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\text{cal}}$ and $\Sigma_{\text{cal}}$ are the mean vector and covariance matrix of the projected calibration set $\Phi(X_{\text{cal}})$, respectively.

\subsection{The Five-Suite Evaluation Framework}
To systematically evaluate OOD detection, we construct five test suites ($N=1000$ context samples each):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ID, Low Noise}: Spectral Mixture (SM) GP samples generated from the training prior with low noise ($\sigma \le 0.001$). This serves as the clean negative test set.
    \item \textbf{ID, High Noise}: SM GP samples from the training prior corrupted by high observation noise ($\sigma \sim 0.4$). This checks whether the detector erroneously fires on noise.
    \item \textbf{Near-OOD}: SM GP samples with frequency parameters shifted outside the training range ($f_{\text{max}} \sim [5.0, 10.0]$).
    \item \textbf{Far-OOD}: SM GP samples with frequency parameters shifted extremely far outside the training range ($f_{\text{max}} \sim 15.0$).
    \item \textbf{Real-World OOD}: Normalized segments of real-world regression time series (e.g., airline passenger counts and minimum daily temperatures).
\end{enumerate}

Our results reveal a critical trade-off that we term the \textit{Noise Robustness Paradox}. In-context prediction error (MSE) and linear representations (PCA-10D) are highly sensitive to out-of-prior data. However, they achieve AUROC scores of $1.000$ and $0.953$ respectively on the \textit{Robustness to Noise} task. This means they cannot distinguish structural prior violation from simple observation noise, triggering false alarms on noisy ID datasets.

Conversely, the joint-fitted UMAP-2D Mahalanobis distance remains close to chance-level performance on noisy ID data (AUROC = $0.471$), demonstrating complete robustness to observation noise. At the same time, it maintains strong detection performance on Near-OOD (AUROC = $1.000$), Far-OOD (AUROC = $1.000$), and Real-World OOD (AUROC = $0.840$). The non-linear projection successfully filters out high-frequency noise variance while preserving the topological features representing prior violation.
